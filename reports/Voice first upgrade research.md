# Make every rubric criterion audible on camera

Bol Ke Bharo already has the hardest parts a judge looks for: a real civic task, two people talking Hindi/Hinglish, a safety check on names and spoken consent. The best use of the next ~48 hours is to make the conversation feel fully hands-free and safe, then make every rubric criterion obvious in the video and README. More models or more languages would earn less. Finish the in-progress hands-free loop, retry ladder and "dobara"/"ruko" commands first. Then add, in order: on-screen labels that make the demo easy to follow, field-tiered confirmation with digit-group read-back, a name guard that tolerates spelling differences, slower speech tuned for elderly users, and only then, as an optional stretch behind a flag, Sarvam code-mix speech-to-text. The reason is that voice alone no longer stands out. One participant counted 141 of 398 projects at a 2026 Berkeley hackathon as voice-first ([Peerlist](https://peerlist.io/naomi_lgbt/articles/i-stayed-for-36-hours-straight-heres-why)). Judges at voice hackathons also reward a working, real-world action with one memorable moment, and they mark down entries without a working demo ([ElevenLabs](https://elevenlabs.io/blog/announcing-the-winners-of-the-elevenlabs-worldwide-hackathon); [AI Tinkerers Lisbon](https://lisbon.aitinkerers.org/hackathons/h_vWDCYNJ82Jg/entries)). Your memorable moment is already built: the system catches a name mismatch and resolves it by voice. The job now is to make it smooth, reliable and impossible to miss. Plan to keep about 8–10 of the 48 hours for the video, README and Wispr note. They decide how the work is read, and an AI pre-screen probably reads them before any human does.

## Five design patterns from production voice stacks

**Turn-taking.** Every major framework ends a turn after a short silence and then waits a little longer before acting. LiveKit's default end-of-turn delay is **0.5 s**. Its bank-IVR example uses **5 s** for slow, structured input ([LiveKit](https://docs.livekit.io/agents/logic/turns/tuning/)). Vapi waits **0.4 s** before speaking ([Vapi](https://docs.vapi.ai/customization/speech-configuration)). OpenAI Realtime has an `idle_timeout_ms` setting (5–30 s) that triggers a response automatically when the user stays silent ([OpenAI](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/realtime-vad)). In the browser, Web Speech with `continuous=false` does its own endpointing ([Chrome](https://developer.chrome.com/blog/voice-driven-web-apps-introduction-to-the-web-speech-api?hl=en)). Old developer reports say Chrome cancels after roughly 7 s of no speech *(unverified, 2015)*. Restarting recognition from `onend` with no guard can loop and crash the tab ([W3C list](https://lists.w3.org/Archives/Public/public-speech-api/2015May/0001.html); [WICG #91](https://github.com/wicg/speech-api/issues/91)). **Suggestion** for the in-progress loop:
- Run half-duplex: either the app is speaking or the mic is on, never both.
- Start listening 250–400 ms after `utterance.onend`.
- Run your own no-input timer of 6–8 s.
- Use a `shouldListen` flag plus a restart counter, and never restart while `speechSynthesis.speaking`.
- Elderly speakers pause mid-answer. Consider `continuous=true` with your own finalize timer that fires about 1.2–1.5 s after the last result. Test this with a real elderly voice, because the value is untested.

**Barge-in.** In the frameworks, about **0.2–0.5 s** of user speech interrupts the agent. LiveKit also resumes playback after a "false interruption" if no transcript arrives within 2 s ([LiveKit](https://docs.livekit.io/agents/logic/turns/tuning/); [Vapi](https://docs.vapi.ai/customization/speech-configuration)). True barge-in in a browser needs echo cancellation and a client-side voice detector such as `@ricky0123/vad-web` ([GitHub](https://github.com/ricky0123/vad)). On laptop speakers it can trigger on the app's own voice ([DEV](https://dev.to/lenajhoffmann/barge-in-vad-and-the-latency-budget-engineering-realtime-voice-3i1b)). **Suggestion:** do not build VAD barge-in this round. The "ruko" command plus a big stop button with an Esc hotkey covers the need. Make the final read-back and consent impossible to interrupt, the same idea as LiveKit's `interruption.enabled=false`, so a cough cannot skip the step that carries legal weight.

**Confirmation.** Google and Amazon both recommend **implicit confirmation for routine values** and **explicit confirmation only for high-stakes or error-prone values**, plus one confirmation of the whole request before the final action ([Google](https://developers.google.com/assistant/conversation-design/confirmations?hl=en); [Alexa](https://developer.amazon.com/en-US/docs/alexa/interaction-model-design/design-the-prompts-for-your-skill.html)). IVR practice is to read numbers back digit by digit. Avaya calls this the only reliable method in languages other than US English ([Avaya](https://support.avaya.com/elmodocs2/ir/15508.htm)). A three-tier scheme is common: accept, confirm, or re-ask, chosen by confidence ([US 8767927](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/8767927)). Chrome's `confidence` value is not standard across browsers ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/SpeechRecognitionAlternative/confidence)). **Suggestion:** pick the tier from format validation plus a per-field `confidence`/`needs_confirmation` value returned by Claude. Don't rely on browser confidence.

**Error recovery.** Google treats no-input as "they didn't hear me", so repeat the question in a shorter form. It treats no-match as "they didn't know what to say", so add examples. Google caps retries at 2–3 consecutive errors; its own pages differ on the exact number ([Google](https://developers.google.com/assistant/conversation-design/errors?authuser=2); [Dialogflow tips](https://developers.google.com/assistant/df-asdk/dialogflow/tips?authuser=5)). Amazon warns against over-apologizing or blaming the user ([Alexa Haus](https://developer.amazon.com/en-US/alexa/alexa-haus/patterns-and-components/patterns-errors)). **Suggestion:** the in-progress ladder should go (1) shorter rephrase, (2) rephrase with an example ("jaise, pandrah August unnees sau pachaas, ya sirf umar"), (3) hand off to the operator: focus the field and pause the mic. Reset the counter on every success. Never say "galat".

**Universal commands and elderly pacing.** Alexa requires stop, cancel and help, and has built-in intents for repeat and previous ([Alexa intents](https://developer.amazon.com/docs/alexa/custom-skills/standard-built-in-intents.html)). Nuance recommends a small set of commands that work at every step ([Nuance](https://docs.nuance.com/nvp-for-speech-suite/appdev/vxml-univ.html)). Older users give up more readily, struggle with fast system speech and cope best with at most three choices ([Vipperla et al.](https://www.cstr.ed.ac.uk/downloads/publications/2012/08-vipperla-2013.pdf)). **Suggestion:** match commands with a keyword check before calling Claude. Show a chip row of the available commands ("dobara · peeche · naam badlo · ruko"). Set TTS rate to about 0.85–0.9 and explicitly pick a Hindi voice from `getVoices()`. Keep prompts to about 10 words or fewer. Because two people speak, the citizen could say "peeche" in passing. A hotkey or an "operator" prefix for navigation commands prevents that; this is a design judgement with no source.

## A 3-minute cut built for both human judges and machine summarizers

Hackathon judging guides agree on the same points. Show the problem in about 20 s. Show the product working within about 90 s ([JetBrains](https://blog.jetbrains.com/ai/2026/06/how-to-win-a-hackathon-notes-from-the-judging-table/)). Never show half-built features or code ([AngelHack](https://angelhack.com/blog/10-tips-to-help-you-rock-your-next-hackathon-demo/)). Production quality does not affect the score ([AI Tinkerers](https://aitinkerers.org/docs/public/video_production_guide.md?markdown=1)). Sponsor tech should be shown in actual use rather than as a logo ([ElevenLabs guide](https://hacks.elevenlabs.io/guide)). Known AI judges first run a compliance check (public repo, working video link). They then turn the video into text and score that text against a human-written rubric ([openHaq](https://devpost.com/software/openhaq); [Devfolio](https://devfolio.co/blog/the-discerning-machine/)). Research shows LLM scores are inconsistent unless the rubric anchors them ([arXiv 2606.05564](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.05564v1)). Scaler's actual review method is not public. **Suggestion:** assume a model will read a transcript of Hindi audio. Burn in English captions and on-screen event labels so it understands what happened.

| Time | Content (suggestion) |
|---|---|
| 0:00–0:20 | Hook: CSC counter scene or a stat card (5.67 lakh CSCs; CAG's 4,713 name-mismatch cases, with source on screen). One line: "Bol ke bharo — the form fills itself while they talk." |
| 0:20–2:10 | **One uncut take**, mic audio audible, small corner webcam, burned-in Hindi + English captions, visible timer. Fields fill live. One mid-sentence correction ("nahi, 1952 nahi, 1954"). One "dobara" and one silence → reprompt. **The name-mismatch catch and spoken resolution** (the wow beat). Digit-group Aadhaar read-back. Final spoken read-back → "haan" → label "Consent captured" → completed form. |
| 2:10–2:40 | Impact: measured voice time vs typed baseline, field accuracy on N test utterances (state N), cost per form. Only numbers you measured. |
| 2:40–3:00 | One-frame architecture, a clip of Wispr Flow dictating a Hinglish prompt or commit, GitHub URL. |

**Suggestion for the README order:** a one-line pitch in English and Hindi with the video link at the top; the problem with sourced numbers; "Try it" with one command plus sample audio, so judges who don't speak Hindi can reproduce the flow; an architecture diagram (mic → STT → Claude extraction / offline rules → validation and name guard → read-back TTS → form); a small eval table with honest sample sizes; a "How we used Wispr Flow" section; known limitations and how Aadhaar/PII data is handled. A judge's write-up says README clarity, architecture diagrams and honest limitations "significantly improve how a project is understood" ([DEV](https://dev.to/amising6/what-i-learned-after-reviewing-many-ai-and-developer-projects-as-a-hackathon-judge-2g06)). Use the rubric's own words in the pain-point and impact text: "voice-first: zero typing by the operator"; "civic services, Hindi/Hinglish, CSCs, elderly pensioners"; "working end to end"; "two-speaker co-pilot with spoken consent". For the Wispr note, give concrete examples: prompts, PR descriptions and debugging notes you dictated in Hinglish, with an honest rough share. Do not invent a percentage.

Risks: field names not verified from the docs, ₹100 signup credits *(unverified)*, sync REST limited to 30 s of audio ([Sarvam STT](https://docs.sarvam.ai/api-reference/speech-to-text/transcribe)), and a new network dependency during the demo. | ~4–6 h |

These do not make the list. Browser VAD barge-in risks the app interrupting itself on laptop speakers. Self-hosting AI4Bharat models is too heavy to set up in 48 h. Bhashini registration may need a review by the Digital India Bhashini Division ([productgrowth.in](https://productgrowth.in/insights/ai-ml/bhashini-api-integration-guide-indian-languages/)). It is worth registering in parallel and calling the design "Bhashini-ready", but don't plan around getting access. Extra language selectors (mr-IN, bn-IN and so on) are cheap for speech recognition but can silently fall back to the wrong TTS voice ([recommended voices list](https://github.com/HadrienGardeur/web-speech-recommended-voices/blob/main/README.md)). Add them only if they are fully tested.

## Conclusion

The judging weights favour feel over features. Each hour on making the conversation smooth, safe and easy to read beats an hour adding a new model. The one sharp moment is a grandmother's name caught before it becomes a rejected pension, settled by voice and confirmed by spoken consent. That moment carries originality, India relevance and voice-first experience together, provided it is captured in one honest, captioned take. Stop adding features about 10 hours before the deadline. Spend that time recording, writing a README in the rubric's own words, and checking that the repo and video link open for a logged-out viewer, since that is the first thing an automated check sees.
