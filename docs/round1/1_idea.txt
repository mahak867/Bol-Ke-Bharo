The problem: Millions of Indians depend on government schemes they cannot apply for on their own. Portals are in English or formal Hindi, need typing, and expect every document to match exactly. So people rely on intermediaries: Common Service Centre (CSC) operators, bank mitras and ASHA workers, who fill forms for them. That last mile breaks. Take an elderly widow applying for her old-age pension. She explains her situation in Awadhi; the operator translates in their head and types it into an English portal. One mismatch, such as "Ramvati" on her Aadhaar and "Ram Wati" in her bank passbook, and the application is rejected weeks later. She comes back again and again, losing a day's wages and bus fare each time. The operator is paid per form, so every slow or rejected form is lost income for them too.

Who has it, where and how often: It affects citizens who need help applying: elderly people, rural women, daily-wage workers, and anyone who doesn't speak the portal's language. It also affects the operators who serve them in villages and small towns across India. It happens every working day. Each operator handles many applications daily, and each one is a spoken conversation that the operator retypes by hand.

The voice-first product: Bol Ke Bharo is a voice co-pilot for the operator. Nobody dictates and nobody types. Operator and citizen simply talk, in Hindi, Hinglish or their dialect.
1. Conversation to form: it listens and fills each field live, with a confidence score.
2. Rejection guard: it catches the mistakes that cause rejections, such as name mismatches, age cutoffs and missing documents. It then asks the citizen by voice, in her language: "Aadhaar par Ramvati hai, bank mein Ram Wati. Kaunsa sahi hai?"
3. Read-back consent: it reads every detail back in her language, and she confirms with "haan". She knows exactly what is filed in her name.
4. Portal autofill: a browser extension types the verified data into the real portal. A human always handles the captcha, the OTP and the final submit.
Audio is never stored, Aadhaar is masked to the last 4 digits, and the product never touches OTPs.

Why voice comes first: The conversation between operator and citizen already happens out loud. Today it is lost and retyped by hand. Bol Ke Bharo captures what is already being said instead of adding a step. For the citizen, voice is the only interface she can use: she cannot read the English portal, but she can hear the read-back and say "haan". Without voice, there is no product, just another form. Every form in India is one schema away: pensions, scholarships, ration cards, crop insurance. Wispr made the keyboard optional for knowledge workers. Bol Ke Bharo makes it optional for the people who fill India's forms for everyone else.
